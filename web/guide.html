WHAT A FILE GIVES AWAY

A photo or a document carries more than what you see in it: where and when it was made, by whom, on what, and what was crossed out on the way. Here is where each kind of file keeps it, how to take it out yourself, and what Dead Drop takes out for you.

Photos and videos

  • Where and when. A phone photo usually records the place it was taken, to a few metres, and the minute; often the altitude and the direction the camera faced as well.
  • On what. The make and model of the phone or camera, the lens, sometimes its serial number and the version of its software.
  • More than the frame. A motion photo, as many Android phones take them, keeps a few seconds of video in the same file, which can show what the picture left out.
  • Videos keep the place and the device in their description, and the time they were recorded.
  • The name. A camera or screenshot names the file after the moment it was made, as in IMG_20261027_143205.jpg.

Yourself: turning location off in the camera stops it being recorded at all. Some share menus offer to leave the location out — on an iPhone, Options at the top of the share sheet — but not the time or the device. Renaming the file before sending takes care of the name.

Word, Excel and PowerPoint

  • Who and when. The author and whoever saved it last, the company and the manager; when it was created, last saved and printed; how many minutes it was edited.
  • Your computer. The path to the template it was made from, with your Windows user name in it; the folder Excel last saved to; the printer it was set up for.
  • Comments and tracked changes, each with the name of whoever made it — deleted text included, still recorded and one click from being read.
  • What is hidden: text formatted hidden, hidden sheets in Excel, hidden slides in PowerPoint. Out of sight on screen; a menu away for whoever opens the file.
  • Where it came from. Identifiers Office stamps on the document, kept through every copy, that match the one you send to the original and to others edited in the same sitting; a document from SharePoint or OneDrive at work keeps the library's address, which names the organisation and the site, and who it was shared with.
  • Pictures inside it keep their own place and time, as if sent on their own.

Yourself: on Windows, File → Info → Check for Issues → Inspect Document, then Remove All for each kind it finds. Go through the tracked changes and comments yourself first: the inspector takes them out without showing you what they said. On a Mac, Word, Excel and PowerPoint have “Remove personal information from this file on save” under Preferences → Security & Privacy.

LibreOffice

  • The same property sheet: author, dates, minutes of editing, and the printer it was set up for, with what its driver keeps.
  • Comments and tracked changes, with names; Calc keeps a log of changes, accepted and rejected ones included.
  • What is hidden: text formatted hidden, directly or by its style; hidden sections; paragraphs and text a field hides; hidden sheets and slides.

Yourself: Tools → Options → LibreOffice → Security → Options, “Remove personal information on saving”; in File → Properties, clear “Apply user data” and press Reset Properties. Edit → Track Changes → Manage → Accept All, then delete the comments.

PDF

  • Who made it and with what: the author, the program it was written in and the one that turned it into a PDF, the dates, and often a title that is the original file's name.
  • Earlier versions. An editor that saves quickly adds its changes to the end of the file and leaves the earlier version in it, properties and all.
  • Comments, highlights and stamps, each with the name of whoever made it.
  • A black box is not a redaction. A rectangle drawn over text hides it from the eye and nothing else: the text is still there to be selected and copied. A redaction marked but not yet applied is the same, until it is applied.

Yourself: in Acrobat, the Redact tool marks the text and Apply removes it; Sanitize Document, in the same place, removes the properties and hidden information. Anything else that draws a box over text is not redacting it.

What Dead Drop does

  • On your device, before encryption. Metadata removal is on unless you switch it off, and happens in your browser; the server never sees the file, cleaned or not.
  • Photos and videos lose place, time and device without being re-encoded, and a motion photo its video; HEIC, AVIF and TIFF leave as JPEG. A video's description is blanked and its times zeroed in place, so it plays as before; the timed tracks some action cameras record stay. A name made by a camera or a screenshot becomes a plain one.
  • Documents lose the property sheet, your user name and folders, the printer, the identifiers that match a copy to the original, and SharePoint's record; pictures inside them lose theirs. A PDF loses its properties in every version kept inside it.
  • Comments, tracked changes and hidden parts are what the document says, so they are not removed silently: the app says which there are and whose names they carry, and asks whether to take them out and send, send as it is, or not send. Taking them out accepts the changes. Hidden sheets and slides, text LibreOffice hides, Excel's comments, a spreadsheet's tracked changes and a redaction never applied it leaves to the program that made them.
  • A document with a password or a digital signature is sent only if you agree: the first cannot be read to be cleaned, and cleaning the second would break the signature.

What no tool takes out

  • What the document says: a name in the text, a letterhead, a signature, a reference number.
  • What the picture shows: a street sign, a reflection, a face, a screen in the background.
  • How you write, when you send, and to whom.
  • What the other person does with it: once they have the file, it is theirs to keep or pass on.
